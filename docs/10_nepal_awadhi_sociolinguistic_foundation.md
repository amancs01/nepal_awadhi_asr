# Why this matters for ASR

Nepal-Awadhi should not be treated as a single undifferentiated target without preserving the variation documented in primary sociolinguistic sources. For ASR, geographic variation, contact-language exposure, speech domain, literacy, and local language attitudes can affect vocabulary, pronunciation, code choice, transcription conventions, and evaluation validity.

This revision uses directly verified facts from Thakur and Yadav (2013), *A Sociolinguistic Survey of Awadhi*, Linguistic Survey of Nepal (LinSuN), Central Department of Linguistics, Tribhuvan University. The report supports verified geographic and lexical variation across surveyed Nepal-Awadhi locations, while also concluding that the surveyed varieties remain mutually intelligible and that observed dialectal variation does not appear to hinder mutual intelligibility. Therefore, this document distinguishes verified variation from a strong mutually unintelligible dialect division, which is not supported.

# Geographic distribution

Source evidence:

Thakur and Yadav (2013) surveyed five Nepal-Awadhi locations:

- Tenuhawa, Rupandehi.
- Jamuni/Maharajgunj, Kapilvastu.
- Thandeukhuri/Gadhwa, Dang.
- Tribhuvan Chowk/Nepalgunj, Banke.
- Gulariya, Bardiya.

Gautam's discussion of cross-border languages reports Awadhi in Nepal's western Terai, listing Nawalparasi, Rupandehi, Kapilvastu, Banke, Bardiya, Dang, Kailali, and Kanchanpur [https://doi.org/10.30687/bhasha/2785-5953/2025/02/003]. Earlier summaries also describe Awadhi as a cross-border Indo-Aryan language in the Terai bordering India [https://benjamins.com/catalog/le.19004.gau].

The 2021 Nepal census thematic material identifies Avadhi/Awadhi as one of Nepal's large mother tongues, with 864,276 mother-tongue speakers and 75,651 second-language speakers in the surfaced report text [https://censusresults.nsonepal.gov.np/downloads/thematic?type=report]. District-level values should still be verified against official district tables before use as sampling numbers.

TU thesis records independently locate Awadhi-speaking respondents in Gadhawa VDC / Deukhuri valley of Dang, Banke district, Rupandehi district, and Bijayanagar in Kapilvastu [TU eLibrary records listed above].

Our design interpretation:

Future sampling should not rely on one district if the research question says Nepal-Awadhi generally. At minimum, corpus planning should preserve district and local-level metadata. If practical, initial sampling should include more than one of the LinSuN-surveyed regions or clearly state that the study is locality-limited.

# LinSuN survey locations

Source evidence:

The primary LinSuN Awadhi survey used five survey locations: Tenuhawa in Rupandehi, Jamuni/Maharajgunj in Kapilvastu, Thandeukhuri/Gadhwa in Dang, Tribhuvan Chowk/Nepalgunj in Banke, and Gulariya in Bardiya (Thakur and Yadav 2013).

The main questionnaire sample was N=60, with 30 male and 30 female respondents. Age categories were 15-29, 30-59, and 60+. Wordlist informants included at least two speakers per survey point. Wordlist speakers were selected to be born in the village or nearby, to be Awadhi mother-tongue speakers, and to not have lived outside the village for extended periods. Wordlist responses were elicited using Nepali prompts and transcribed in IPA (Thakur and Yadav 2013).

Our design interpretation:

The future corpus protocol should record at least district/locality, age band, sex/gender category, literacy/education, mother-tongue status, local-residence history, and contact-language profile. The wordlist criteria are especially relevant for controlled speech sampling because they directly address local origin/residence and mother-tongue status.

# Within-Nepal variation

Source evidence:

Thakur and Yadav (2013) used a 210-word list for lexical comparison across the five survey points. Reported lexical similarity across the five survey points is approximately 78%-89%. The report concludes that the surveyed varieties remain mutually intelligible and that the observed dialectal variation does not appear to hinder mutual intelligibility.

This directly verifies geographic/lexical variation. It does not support a strong claim that Nepal-Awadhi consists of mutually unintelligible dialect divisions.

No primary fact in this revision establishes a detailed ASR-ready phonological map or pronunciation typology. Pronunciation/phonological differences remain to be checked directly in the report and any related linguistic descriptions.

Our design interpretation:

There is enough evidence to preserve locality metadata and avoid treating one district as automatically representative. There is not enough evidence to predefine strong dialect groups for ASR modeling. The safest future reporting approach is aggregate Nepal-Awadhi plus district/region metadata, with regional subgroup analyses only if sample size and evidence support them.

# Nepali contact

Source evidence:

Thakur and Yadav (2013) report direct multilingualism evidence among main respondents: Nepali knowledge is reported by 70% of male respondents and 33% of female respondents. Children also show substantial reported knowledge of Nepali, with Nepali associated with schooling, towns, and cities.

This establishes Nepali as a highly relevant contact language at the level of reported language knowledge and social domains. It does not establish frequent utterance-level code-switching, acoustic influence, or ASR donor effectiveness.

# Hindi contact

Source evidence:

Thakur and Yadav (2013) report direct multilingualism evidence among main respondents: Hindi knowledge is reported by 83% of male respondents and 47% of female respondents. Children also show substantial reported knowledge of Hindi, with Hindi associated with markets, India, films, and television.

This establishes Hindi as a highly relevant contact language at the level of reported language knowledge and social domains. It does not prove that Hindi is the best ASR donor, and it does not quantify Hindi lexical normalization in Nepal-Awadhi ASR errors.

# Other language contact

Source evidence:

Earlier Phase 0D sources suggested that English and Tharu also occur in the broader Awadhi contact ecology, with English associated with schooling and Tharu with local society in summarized LinSuN discussion [https://doi.org/10.30687/bhasha/2785-5953/2025/02/003]. Bhojpuri and Urdu are locally relevant in Nepal's Terai language ecology, and the 2021 census thematic material lists Bhojpuri, Urdu, Hindi, Tharu, Bajjika, and other Indo-Aryan languages among Nepal's language ecology [https://censusresults.nsonepal.gov.np/downloads/thematic?type=report].

However, this revision does not yet have direct Awadhi-specific primary-report values for Bhojpuri, Urdu, Tharu, or English contact comparable to the Nepali/Hindi figures above. Their role in actual Nepal-Awadhi speech should remain PARTIAL or UNKNOWN until directly verified.

# Multilingualism and language use

Source evidence:

The primary LinSuN report directly supports multilingualism involving Nepali and Hindi among Awadhi respondents. It also distinguishes domains: Nepali is associated with schooling/towns, while Hindi is associated with markets, India, films, and television (Thakur and Yadav 2013).

This is evidence for language knowledge and domain-linked language exposure. It is not equivalent to measured utterance-level code-switching frequency. The current project should not infer that a speaker who knows Nepali or Hindi necessarily code-switches frequently in recorded Awadhi speech.

# What the sources actually establish

- Thakur and Yadav (2013) surveyed five Nepal-Awadhi locations: Tenuhawa/Rupandehi, Jamuni-Maharajgunj/Kapilvastu, Thandeukhuri-Gadhwa/Dang, Tribhuvan Chowk-Nepalgunj/Banke, and Gulariya/Bardiya.
- The main questionnaire sample was N=60, with 30 male and 30 female respondents and age categories 15-29, 30-59, and 60+.
- The lexical comparison used a 210-word list.
- Wordlist informants included at least two speakers per survey point; they were selected as local or nearby-born Awadhi mother-tongue speakers without extended residence outside the village.
- Wordlist responses were elicited using Nepali prompts and transcribed in IPA.
- Reported lexical similarity across the five survey points is approximately 78%-89%.
- The report concludes that the surveyed varieties remain mutually intelligible and that observed dialectal variation does not appear to hinder mutual intelligibility.
- Nepali and Hindi are both strongly supported as relevant sociolinguistic contact languages: main respondents report Nepali knowledge at 70% male / 33% female and Hindi knowledge at 83% male / 47% female.

# What they do NOT establish

- They do not establish an ASR-ready Nepal-Awadhi corpus.
- They do not establish a strong mutually unintelligible dialect division within Nepal-Awadhi.
- They do not establish that code-switching is frequent in actual recorded speech.
- They do not establish that Hindi or Nepali is a better ASR donor.
- They do not establish that WER/CER alone can capture language-fidelity errors.
- They do not establish a complete phonological/pronunciation map for ASR error analysis in this revision.

# Implications for future corpus sampling

Source evidence:

The primary report documents five survey points across Rupandehi, Kapilvastu, Dang, Banke, and Bardiya, a 210-word lexical comparison, approximately 78%-89% lexical similarity, mutual intelligibility across surveyed varieties, and local-born/mother-tongue/non-extended-absence criteria for wordlist informants (Thakur and Yadav 2013).

Our design interpretation:

- Sample multiple regions if the claim is about Nepal-Awadhi broadly.
- Preserve district/locality metadata even if reporting aggregate Nepal-Awadhi results.
- Avoid one-family or one-network sampling.
- Include local-residence and mother-tongue criteria for controlled speech, modeled on the LinSuN wordlist criteria where appropriate.
- Document multilingual background rather than excluding multilingual speakers.
- Separate read/controlled and spontaneous/natural speech.
- Maintain speaker-disjoint train/dev/test splits.
- Report one-region samples as one-region evidence only.
- Consider balancing by sex/gender, age band, literacy/education, and contact-language exposure if feasible.

# Implications for our research questions

The primary cross-border question remains plausible but must preserve district/region metadata. Because LinSuN reports mutual intelligibility despite lexical variation, the project should avoid framing Nepal-Awadhi as internally split into strongly separate dialects unless further evidence supports that.

The Hindi/Nepali donor question survives and is now better grounded sociolinguistically. Both Hindi and Nepali should be treated as HIGH-confidence contact languages for Nepal-Awadhi. This does not imply either language will be a good ASR donor; donor behavior remains an experiment to be designed later.

The language-fidelity question remains plausible but must not assume frequent code-switching. It should be framed around empirically testable substitution/normalization patterns, with the annotation scheme postponed until later Phase 0 work.

# Remaining uncertainty

- Exact full lexical-similarity matrix values should be transcribed into a table if the project needs site-by-site sampling weights.
- Whether the LinSuN report documents pronunciation/phonological variation relevant to ASR remains to be checked in detail.
- Frequency of utterance-level code-switching in natural Nepal-Awadhi speech remains UNKNOWN.
- The role of Bhojpuri, Urdu, Tharu, and English in actual Nepal-Awadhi speech remains PARTIAL/UNKNOWN.
- Whether any LinSuN audio, wordlist recordings, or field recordings exist remains UNKNOWN.
